# AI tells

Use at Phases 5, 5b and 6, and again in the Phase 7 checks, on every document.

Selectors notice these patterns, and they read as effort outsourced. Most of them are also symptoms: they appear where a specific fact has been replaced by a generic, flattering statement that could describe anyone. So fix the cause. Replace the pattern with the specific fact it was standing in for, or cut the sentence. Removing the surface pattern while leaving the generic claim in place fixes nothing.

How to use this list:
- Judge clusters rather than single hits. One "crucial" proves nothing, while three of these patterns in one paragraph means the paragraph needs rewriting.
- Rewrite the sentence rather than swapping the word. A synonym standing in for an overused word keeps the empty claim, and the synonym becomes the next tell.
- Do not rough the text up to look human. Perfect grammar, a mix of formal and casual registers, and ordinary transition words are not tells, and adding errors makes a document worse rather than more believable.
- Quotations and the person's own verbatim words are exempt. Their captured voice decides what sounds like them.
- Write in the market's English. Several assistants default to American English, so a British or European candidate's CV in American spelling is a tell in itself.

The patterns, with specimens marked as specimens rather than as models to follow.

Content:
- Inflated significance, where something routine is said to represent a broader trend, shift or legacy. Specimen: "played a pivotal role in shaping the evolving landscape". Say what changed and by how much.
- Promotional language, the register of a press release or a brochure. Specimen: "renowned for an unwavering commitment to excellence".
- Canned credibility, claiming recognition, coverage or presence instead of naming it. Specimen: "widely recognised across the industry". Name the award, the publication or the audience.
- Analysis tacked onto a fact with an "-ing" tail that interprets it. Specimen: "Cut costs by 12%, highlighting a strong commitment to efficiency." The fact stands on its own, so cut the tail.
- Vague attribution. Specimen: "Stakeholders praised the approach." Say who, or cut it.
- Vague connection, where the role goes unstated. Specimen: "associated with the growth of the business". Say the role: led, owned, advised, contributed to.
- The challenges-then-optimism close. Specimen: "Despite these challenges, the organisation is well placed for future growth."

Language:
- Assistant vocabulary in clusters. These words have been heavily overused by assistants since 2023: additionally (opening a sentence), align with, boasts (meaning has), bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (as a verb), interplay, intricate, key (as an adjective), landscape (as an abstract noun), meticulous, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore, valuable, vibrant. The list shifts with each model generation, and a word being on it says nothing about its synonyms.
- Avoiding plain "is" and "has". Specimen: "serves as the lead for the programme" where "leads the programme" is meant. Prefer the plain verb: led, wrote, used, tried, moved, rather than spearheaded, authored, utilised, attempted, relocated.
- Contrarian openers. Specimen: "It's not just X, it's Y."
- Antithesis pairs, where a claim is made by denying its opposite. Specimen: "I don't manage projects, I deliver outcomes." Pick one half and say it.
- Threes by reflex: three one-word sentences in a row, or lists that always run to three. Specimen: "Strategic. Decisive. Relentless."
- Stacked short sentences placed back to back for rhythm. Each may be fine alone; in a row they read as constructed cadence. Join them with "but", "because" or a comma where that reads more naturally.
- The self-rating clause, where a sentence stops to tell the reader how good the thing it just described was. Specimen: "I led the migration, which was a significant achievement." If a clause only rates the clause before it, cut it.
- The diligence tail, a close variant that credits the writer with intent or care rather than rating the result. Specimens: "..., and nobody else had noticed", "..., deliberately", "..., on purpose". Delete the tail; if nothing factual is lost, it was a rating.
- The closing maxim, a final sentence that restates the paragraph as a slogan. Specimen: "Authenticity beats polish every time." If the sentence would work unchanged on a poster, it is decoration.
- The neat concluding thesis that wraps a section in a bow.
- Hollow enthusiasm: passionate, thrilled, deeply committed, with nothing behind it.
- Uniform sentence rhythm, where every sentence runs to the same length.
- Corporate filler: synergy, leverage as a verb, spearheaded, results-driven, dynamic, rockstar, ninja, hustle.

Formatting:
- Em-dash overuse. A comma, a colon, a full stop or a bracket does the same work. The rule is overuse rather than an outright ban, because a person who uses them naturally should keep their own punctuation. Watch this one closely on Claude, which a July 2026 study found still uses them more than professional writers.
- Bold used for emphasis throughout, and lists where every item opens with a bold label and a colon.
- Title Case In Every Heading, and emoji used as bullets or heading decoration.
- Leftover assistant text and unfilled placeholders. Specimens: "Here is your updated profile:", "I hope this helps", "[Company Name]".

These patterns are drawn from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" field guide as it stood in September 2026, research on assistant vocabulary and grammar published in 2025, and lessons recorded while this kit was built. Review the list when a new generation of assistants arrives, because the vocabulary moves.
